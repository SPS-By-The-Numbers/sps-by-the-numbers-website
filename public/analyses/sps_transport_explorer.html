Key Inflection Points

2021-22 Cap Trap (−$27.7M): Formula calculated $53.9M but COVID-year D.2 of $18.3M capped the allocation at $20M. The mechanism designed to prevent overpayment became the instrument of maximum underpayment.

2022-23 Worst Year (−$37.1M): SPS spent $65.3M while D.2 used 2021-22’s partial-recovery figure as ceiling. SPS self-funded 57 cents of every transportation dollar.

2023-24 Route Restructuring (+76 net sped routes): SPS added 141 new Special Ed routes and dropped 65, a near-complete rebuild of the sped routing system. Special Ed routes grew from 223 to 299 (+34%) while sped riders grew from 1,881 to 2,290 (+22%). Riders per sped route fell from 8.4 to 7.7 and has declined every year since. The formula recovered approximately $420K of the ~$7.6M in new route operating costs — about 5 cents per dollar.

2024-25 Partial Recovery (−$23.0M): D.2 now reflects real expenditures. The cap is no longer artificially low. The remaining gap is structural: Basic ridership has not recovered to pre-COVID levels (~9,200 vs ~18,000 in 2019-20), costing approximately $23M in formula allocation annually.

The D.2 Lag: STARS uses prior-year F-196 actuals as the expenditure cap. When costs jump sharply, D.2 understates what SPS spent — tightening the cap at the worst moment. The restructuring in 2023-24 raised the cost base that D.2 will reflect in future years, partially closing the lag.

Shaky AI Analyses

⚠ Important disclaimer before reading anything below. These analyses are preliminary, based on incomplete data, and depend heavily on assumptions that have not been validated against SPS operational data. They are intended to generate hypotheses for further investigation, not to support policy decisions. Several key inputs — per-route costs, student address distributions, walk zone sizes, IEP transport obligations, and actual count-day attendance — are estimated or assumed rather than measured. Results could change substantially with better data. Do not cite these figures without independent verification.
Global Assumptions Underlying All Analyses
These assumptions apply across multiple questions below. Each represents a potential source of error.
  • Operating cost per route assumed uniform across all types (~$571/day). Special Ed routes with aides or specialized vehicles almost certainly cost more.
  • Per-route ridership derived from quarterly summary On Buses ÷ route count (~51.5 riders/route for Basic). This assumes ridership is uniformly distributed across routes, which is unlikely.
  • Walk zone absorption rates (what share of students live close enough to their new school to not need a bus) are estimated at 25–75% with no supporting address-level data.
  • New neighborhood routes assumed to average ~25 students each — roughly half the current option school average — reflecting geographic dispersion. Actual routing would depend on address data.
  • Transit pass cost assumed at $900/student/year. Actual SPS transit pass cost not verified.
  • Geographic distribution of option and HCC school students assumed roughly city-wide. In reality, HCC enrollment likely skews toward certain neighborhoods.
  • Formula calculations use 2025-26 coefficients throughout. Coefficients change annually — results would differ in other years.
  • Gifted route riders assumed to count in Special Program CSPC per OSPI General Instructions. This has not been independently confirmed against the 1022E form itself.
  • Count-day consistency assumed at ~80% (students present on count day). Actual SPS attendance data not reviewed.
  • Route length and operational scheduling constraints (driver hours, school start windows) are not modeled except where noted.

2021-22 is a severe outlier. The COVID year produced a regression on abnormal ridership data: Basic coefficient halved (0.394 vs ~0.67 in normal years), Special tripled (0.303 vs ~0.11), and the land area coefficient went negative (−0.169). 1Exploration: Does shrinking or expanding the walk zone increase or decrease STARS funding?
▼
Est. budget impact: $-7.2M to +$7.4M/yrConfidence: LOW
🔎 Tentative Finding
Shrinking the walk zone (busing more students) appears to generate positive net returns because the marginal formula value per added Basic rider (~$2,650/yr at 2025-26 coefficients) substantially exceeds the marginal operating cost of adding stops to existing routes (~$51–640/student/yr). The range is wide because it depends heavily on whether new riders can be served by adding stops to existing routes or require entirely new routes. Expanding the walk zone is negative in all scenarios — formula loss per removed rider exceeds operating savings even when full route elimination is achieved ($99,925 saved vs ~$136,700 lost in formula per route eliminated).
📊 Data & Methods
Formula marginal value computed analytically from 2025-26 1026A coefficients. Operating cost estimated from F-196 total expenditure ÷ routes ÷ days (~$571/route/day). Stop addition cost estimated at $40/day driver time ÷ 10 students per stop = ~$51/student/yr marginal cost. New route cost ~$1,960/student/yr.
⚠ Key Assumptions
  • 70–90% of walk-zone-adjacent students can be served by adding stops to existing routes rather than new routes — this depends entirely on whether existing routes pass near those students, which is unknown without GIS analysis
  • Stop addition cost estimated at $40/day extra driver time, divided across ~10 students per stop — not verified against SPS driver contracts
  • Route length and timing constraints are not modeled — if existing routes are at capacity, stop additions may trigger new routes instead
  • Count-day consistency assumed at 80%
✅ Steps to Verify
  • SPS has address-level enrollment data and GIS stop locations — model which walk-zone students are near existing routes vs. would require new routes
  • Review current route timing windows for slack capacity to add stops
  • Check state minimum walk zone requirements (SPS cannot set zones below state minimums)
  • Review driver contract constraints on route length and shift time
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • The entire positive result depends on existing routes passing near walk-zone students — if they don’t, most would require new routes, reducing the net benefit substantially (though it remains positive even at 50% new routes)
  • Adding stops may push routes past school start/end timing windows, triggering new routes rather than stop additions
  • The average distance term in the formula decreases slightly as shorter-distance stops are added — this partially offsets the CBPC gain (modeled as small but not verified)
2Exploration: What if option and HCC schools were made into neighborhood schools?
▼
Est. budget impact: −$5.6M to −$7.2M/yrConfidence: LOW
🔎 Tentative Finding
Converting option and HCC schools to neighborhood attendance-area schools makes SPS’s financial position worse in every scenario tested. The operating cost of building new neighborhood-scale routes (many short routes, few students each) exceeds savings from eliminating option routes. Net losses range from roughly −$5.6M to −$7.2M depending on walk zone absorption assumptions (25–50%). The formula effect is mixed but generally negative due to loss of Gifted CSPC riders and the dispersion of students across many short new routes.
📊 Data & Methods
13 option/HCC schools, ~5,350 students total. Route counts from Fall quarterly detail pages. Operating cost at $571/route/day. Walk zone absorption modeled at 25–50% (no supporting data). New neighborhood routes assumed at 25 students/route average. Formula uses 2025-26 coefficients.
⚠ Key Assumptions
  • Walk zone absorption (what % of redistributed students end up within walking distance of their new neighborhood school) assumed at 25–50% — the single most consequential unknown, with no SPS address-level data to support it
  • New neighborhood routes average 25 students each — roughly half the current option school average — reflecting geographic dispersion after redistribution
  • Geographic distribution of option school families assumed roughly city-wide — HCC enrollment likely skews toward certain neighborhoods, which could significantly change walk zone absorption
  • Sped transport assumed unchanged (follows the student via IEP)
✅ Steps to Verify
  • SPS has address-level enrollment data that would allow actual walk zone calculation to each student’s neighborhood school — this would replace the 25–50% assumption with a real number
  • Routing software (Versatrans) could model actual new route requirements
  • Review legal requirements for HCC transportation under RCW 28A.185 (state highly capable statute)
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • Walk zone absorption dominates the result — a 10-point change in that assumption shifts the net by ~$1–2M
  • HCC schools are centrally located relative to a city-wide draw — students travel from across Seattle precisely because the program is not neighborhood-based. Walk zone absorption upon redistribution to neighborhood schools could be lower than the city average, since HCC families may live far from both the HCC school and their neighborhood school
  • Political and legal feasibility of eliminating option programs is entirely outside this analysis

🔎 Tentative Finding
The 2023-24 expansion was almost entirely Special Ed routes (+76 net: 141 new, 65 dropped). Basic routes were essentially unchanged (+1 net). New Special Ed routes have longer average distances (2.49 mi vs 2.20 mi for continued routes) and lower student counts. The pattern — district-wide, shallow increases at 53+ schools, most gaining just 1–2 routes — is consistent with IEP compliance expansion. 📊 Data & Methods
Route IDs from Fall quarterly detail pages 2022-23 and 2023-24. Continuity analysis: 390 route IDs survived unchanged, 104 dropped, 182 new. New routes identified by ID not present in prior year. Distance averages from route detail distance field. Formula recovery calculated from CSPC change × 2025-26 coefficients.
⚠ Key Assumptions
  • Route ID changes treated as genuinely new routes, not renumbering — the Ingraham example shows 8 of 9 route IDs were retired and replaced even where net count changed only slightly, so this may overstate “newness”
  • The compliance interpretation is inferred from the data pattern — no SPS policy document, IEP audit, or board directive has been reviewed
  • Operating cost per sped route assumed at the district average ($571/day) — sped routes likely cost more due to aides and specialized vehicles
✅ Steps to Verify
  • Ask SPS transportation staff what drove the 2023-24 expansion — was there an IEP audit, OCR complaint, or board directive?
  • Review SPS board meeting minutes from 2022-23 for transportation policy changes
  • Check whether OCR or OSPI issued compliance correspondence related to special ed transportation
  • Check if the route ID system was intentionally rebuilt (explaining mass ID retirement) vs. IDs changed for operational reasons
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • The compliance hypothesis is plausible but unverified — the expansion could reflect enrollment growth, a new contractor, building moves (Mercer TEMP CONST), or other factors
  • We cannot tell from route data alone whether new routes serve genuinely new transport needs or replace informal arrangements (parent reimbursement, contracted individual runs) previously off the route detail system
  • The mass route ID retirement at Ingraham (1 of 9 IDs survived) suggests a system-wide routing rebuild, which could mean “new” routes are operationally the same routes with new numbers
4Exploration: What happens to STARS funding when Basic riders shift to other transport categories?
▼
~−$1,700 to −$2,650 per shifted student/yrConfidence: MEDIUM
🔎 Tentative Finding
Moving a student off a Basic route always reduces STARS funding. How much depends on where they go. The Basic Program coefficient (0.665) is the dominant term in the formula. Any shift that removes a rider from the Basic CBPC count incurs a formula loss of ~$2,650/student/yr. If the student gains a count in the Special CSPC (Special Ed, Gifted, Early Ed, Homeless — all count in CSPC per OSPI instructions), approximately $948/student is recovered, for a net loss of ~$1,700/student. If the student leaves the formula entirely (transit pass, walk zone, parent transport), the full $2,650/student is lost with no recovery.

Practical implication: There is no category shift that improves funding. Every destination category is worth less to the formula than Basic. The formula is calibrated to maximize value for students on standard Basic routes — any specialization or removal from district buses reduces the allocation.

Scale example: The post-COVID decline in Basic ridership from ~18,000 to ~9,200 (~8,800 fewer Basic riders) cost approximately 8,800 × $2,650 = ~$23.3M in formula allocation annually — roughly equal to the observed funding gap. This is not a coincidence; the ridership decline is the primary structural driver of the gap.

⚠ Key Assumptions
  • CSPC gain per student ($948) computed at current CSPC=4,256 using 2025-26 coefficients — this changes at different rider counts and in different years
  • Gifted, Early Ed, and Homeless riders assumed to count in CSPC per OSPI General Instructions — not independently confirmed against the 1022E submission form
  • The −$1,700/student net figure holds only when the destination category adds to CSPC; it assumes rider counts stay in the formula-relevant range
✅ Steps to Verify
  • Obtain OSPI Form 1022E instructions to confirm how each program type (Gifted, Early Ed, Homeless, Bilingual) is classified in CBPC vs. CSPC
  • Ask SPS transportation staff how they classify riders on their 1022E submission
  • Cross-check the CBPC and CSPC values on the 1026A against the quarterly summary On Buses counts by category
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • The 1022E classification of Gifted riders as CSPC is the single most consequential unverified assumption in this analysis
  • The formula uses annual averages (CBPC = avg of three count days) — students who ride inconsistently contribute less than a full unit to the count
  • The "$23M gap = $23M ridership decline" observation is suggestive but not causal — costs also rose substantially over the same period
