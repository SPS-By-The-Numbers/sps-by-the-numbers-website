Washington State Bussing Analysis

Twelve years of how the state's 315 school districts pay — and don't quite get paid back — for student transportation. OSPI revenue and expenditure data joined to district enrollment, with the rankings you'd expect and the ones you wouldn't. All charts default to actuals (what districts actually received and spent); the district-by-district scatter has a toggle for budget figures.

The shape of the gap

Year by year, the median district falls short

Across the past twelve school years, the statewide weighted gap between transportation revenue and expenditure (actuals) has had a median of −$42 per pupil, with a typical range (IQR) of −$49 to −$26 per pupil. The worst year was 2013-14 at −$106; the best was 2018-19 at +$41, the year after the McCleary funding ramp began. Per-pupil spending has roughly doubled over the period, from $432 to $808 per K-12 FTE.

A district by itself, with two peers for scale

Seattle Public Schools, ten years of student transportation

Seattle is the only Washington district that places dead last on every operational efficiency measure in OSPI's standard 21-district cohort. But "Seattle is just different" is a thin defense — so this tab brings in two peer districts that share Seattle's KPI cohort: Tacoma, the next-worst on cost-per-rider, and Federal Way, the cohort's efficiency leader. The three together let us ask which parts of Seattle's story are about COVID, which are about geography, and which need a different explanation.

The cleanest framing: Tacoma's basic-program ridership collapsed across COVID at almost exactly Seattle's rate, but Tacoma transports its remaining riders at $3,554 each, while Seattle pays $8,652. Whatever caused Seattle's cost explosion, it's not just "Seattle lost riders." Federal Way, by contrast, lost almost no riders and runs a cost-per-rider close to the cohort floor.

F-196 expenditure vs. STARS allocation, three districts
For each district: solid line = F-196 transportation expenditure (what was actually spent), dotted line = the STARS 1026A allocation (the state's formula-driven funding). When the gap between them widens, the district is spending well above what the state's formula says they should need.

Note the structural asymmetry visible here: Tacoma and Federal Way each match their STARS allocation closely — their F-196 expenditure tracks within a few million dollars of what OSPI's formula says they should spend. Seattle's F-196 expenditure has run $20–30 million above its STARS allocation since 2022-23. Seattle is the only one of the three with a persistent and growing gap between what the state pays for and what's actually being spent.

Cost per rider, 2024-25, vs the 21-district KPI cohort
From the April 2026 OSPI KPI Technical Assistance paper. The cohort is the 20 districts closest to Seattle in total ridership. Federal Way sits near the high end of the tight $1.5K–$3K band; Tacoma is the only district inside the band that's clearly elevated; Seattle sits more than 2× past Tacoma.
Basic riders per basic bus, 2024-25, vs the same cohort
The operational counterpart. Federal Way leads the cohort at 127 riders per bus; Tacoma sits in the lower-middle at 62; Seattle is at the bottom at 39.5. Seattle and Tacoma are both urban districts with school choice — the gap between them deserves more explanation than "geography."

A second-order observation: OSPI's efficiency framework has a hole for big urban districts

OSPI's Efficiency Detail Report rates each district against a cohort of similarly-shaped districts — same approximate size, ridership, and geography. The Relative Efficiency Rating is meant to be the headline accountability number. But for the three districts on this page, the cohort logic produces very different outcomes:

Two of the three districts on this page (Seattle and Tacoma) have no cohort to compare against in OSPI's structural framework — both are alone in a "cohort" containing only themselves, and the rating mechanically reports 100%. Federal Way, with a real four-district cohort, gets a meaningful 97.6% rating. The KPI cohort tables (the $1,565-to-$8,652 range we saw above) are a useful comparison — but they live in a separate document that doesn't feed back into the formal efficiency score.

Behind the numbers

Methodology & sources

Data: OSPI SAFS F-196 (actuals) and F-195 (budget), as ingested by the SPS-By-The-Numbers data-tools project into BigQuery (safs_f19x.general_fund_revenues and safs_f19x.general_fund_expenditures). District enrollment from OSPI's historical enrollment summary workbook, summed across grades K-12 (FTE, includes ALE).

Transportation revenue is defined as any revenue line where revenue_code MOD 100 = 99 — the "pupil transportation" program tag. Transportation expenditure is the sum of activity codes 51-59 (Pupil Transportation activities), with Program 99 available as an alternate definition. The two definitions agree closely: median difference $0, with about 5% of district-years showing meaningful divergence (mostly special-ed transportation).

What this dataset cannot show: Special-ed transportation cannot be cleanly separated from general transportation in this data — it appears that districts code transportation lines either entirely under Program 99 or entirely under their special-ed program (e.g. Program 21), and the program × activity intersection is sparse. Per-rider cost would be more informative than per-pupil cost, but ridership counts are not in the OSPI workbook used here.

Cleaning: 62 of 6,648 source rows were dropped: one with a corrupt negative ccddd (likely a sign-overflow bug upstream) and 61 with ccddd codes ending in 9xx that did not join to the district domain table — these are typically state schools, juvenile facilities, or charter authorizers rather than regular K-12 districts.

Color palette: A restrained editorial set of navy / teal / olive / gold / red / plum / ink, sequential by year for the scatter; semantic red and olive for shortfall and surplus. Shape variation (circle vs. diamond for actuals vs. budget, and hollow circle vs. grey square for pre- vs. post-COVID) is used as a redundant encoding alongside color.
