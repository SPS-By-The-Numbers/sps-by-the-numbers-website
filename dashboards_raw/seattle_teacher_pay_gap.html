1The gap is widening — and flat raises are why

A flat percentage raise gives the same proportional bump to everyone — which means a larger dollar bump to those already paid most. Repeated annually, the absolute distance between the lowest- and highest-paid teachers grows even when the ratio holds steady. Seattle's record shows exactly that.

The tell-tale signature of flat raises

From 2013–14 to 2024–25 the dollar gap between the top quintile (Q5) and bottom quintile (Q1) means grew from $42,213 to $58,114 (+38%). Their pay ratio (top quintile Q5 divided by bottom quintile Q1) actually narrowed over the same span — from 1.87× to 1.71× — because the bottom rose faster in percentage terms (the bottom quintile +70% vs the top quintile +55%), as state minimum-salary floors lifted starting pay after McCleary. So the relative gap compressed while the absolute dollar gap widened. The reason the dollars diverge is the raise mechanism: Seattle's contract raises are flat across-the-board percentages — the CBA adds a single uniform percentage to the entire salary schedule each year (7% in 2022–23, 4%, 3%, and 2.5% in 2025–26; "will be added to the salary schedule," SEA–SPS CBA §A.6 — plus a flat 10.5% in 2018–19). A flat percentage on a higher senior base is always more dollars, so it widens the absolute gap by construction — which is what the progressive redo in §3 is designed to bend.

A note on grouping: this trend uses equal-count fifths (the lowest-paid 20% of teachers vs the highest-paid 20%), because that is what tracks a widening gap over time — fixed-dollar bands can't, since teachers migrate up out of them as pay rises. Sections 2 and 3 use equal-width $15k salary bands, which are the natural unit for a single year's distribution and for designing bracket-by-bracket raises.

2The 2024–25 salary distribution, by salary band

Seattle's 2,661 classroom teachers (duties 31–34, ≥0.8 FTE) sorted into five equal-width $15k salary bands from $70k to $145k — cut by salary range, not by headcount, so each band holds however many teachers fall in it. The shape is the point: teachers pile up at the top of the schedule — the highest band (B5, $130–145k) holds the most teachers (29%), the lowest (B1) the fewest (8%).

3What a progressive redo of the 2022 contract would have looked like

The 2022–25 contract added a flat percentage to the entire salary schedule each year — 7% (2022–23), 4% (2023–24), 3% (2024–25) — confirmed verbatim in the contract (“will be added to the salary schedule,” SEA–SPS CBA, §A.6). Below, the same three raises are re-run as a progressive schedule, holding each year’s total raise pool identical to the flat version (cost-neutral to the dollar). Teachers are grouped into five equal $15k salary bands (B1 $70–85k … B5 $130–145k, by 2024–25 base salary). Use the controls to set how much is trimmed from the top two bands — everything recomputes live.

The exchange rate: why a small trim at the top buys a big boost at the bottom

The top band (B5) holds 29% of all teachers and they’re the highest paid, so its total payroll is 6.4× the bottom band’s. A percentage point is therefore worth far more at the bottom. Concretely: 0.5% trimmed from B5 funds ~3.2% added to B1 alone, or about +0.74% spread across the bottom two bands (B1 + B2 hold a lot more teachers). The trims you set below are redistributed to B1 + B2 — with a control to slope how much each gets — while the middle band (B3) stays at the flat rate.

Trims max 3.0% (the smallest annual raise); beyond that a band’s raise turns negative. The slope sets how the freed money splits between the bottom two bands, cost-neutrally.

Sources & method. OSPI S-275 certificated personnel reporting, Seattle (CCDDD 17001), 2013–14 → 2024–25. Population: classroom teachers — duty root codes 31 (Elementary Homeroom), 32 (Secondary), 33 (Other Teacher), 34 (Elementary Specialist) — identified by each person's is_major assignment, restricted to ≥0.8 FTE with a valid salary (2,661 teachers in 2024–25). Salary basis is base "total final salary" per 1.0 FTE; supplemental (TRI), insurance, and mandatory benefits shown separately and not included in the raise pool. Section 1 measures the gap over time with equal-count fifths (the lowest- vs highest-paid 20% of teachers), because that is what tracks a widening gap over time; sections 2–3 group teachers into five equal-width $15k salary bands ($70–145k, by 2024–25 base salary), so band headcounts differ. The interactive redistribution re-runs the actual 2022–25 raises (7/4/3) each year cost-neutrally, trimming the top two bands (B4, B5) by the amounts selected and reallocating the proceeds as an equal point-boost to the bottom two bands (B1, B2); it is illustrative and not modeled against the SEA schedule’s step/lane structure. Because “total final salary” reflects actual payroll, part-year teachers can fall into the lowest band, which understates rather than overstates the true schedule floor. Nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted. Raise structure: the 2018–19 (10.5%) and 2022–25 (7%/4%/3%) Seattle contracts — which govern the raises over the analyzed period — were independently reported as across-the-board percentage increases for certificated teachers; the 2022 contract added a one-time flat $1,500 bonus for the lowest-paid hourly classified staff (instructional assistants, office staff), not the certificated teachers analyzed here. The 2025–26 successor contract (ratified after this data window, not reflected in the figures) applies a flat 2.5% to the whole schedule (CBA §A.6), i.e. the same across-the-board structure.
