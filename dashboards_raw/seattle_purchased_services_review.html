1The short answer

The flyer's numbers are accurate — they reconcile to this independent F-196/F-195 analysis to the dollar. But the interpretations it pushes do not survive the detail. On the readings that matter, the flyer's direction is likely not correct.

The flyer's conclusions fail in five places, each at equal weight — three are misreadings of what the spending is, two are flaws in the flyer's own math:

  • "Purchased services — the great mystery… it's not clear what the funds are for." → Not supported. The spend is fully identifiable from the same F-196 reports the flyer cites. In 2024–25 it is the ~$57M yellow-bus contract, ~$48M Districtwide Support (utilities, insurance, property, IT), and ~$42M special-education contracted instruction & private-placement tuition (NCES 569 Tuition $22M + 322 Contracted Educational Staff $11M). There is no mystery — there is a breakdown (§6–§7).
  • "Redirect purchased services → +197/98/22 staff." → Not supported. This treats the money as discretionary. Most of it is not: transportation is required, special-education tuition and contracted therapists serve specific IEP students and are mandated under IDEA, and utilities/insurance keep buildings open. The slice that is freely convertible to classroom teachers is a fraction of the $179M.
  • "Higher MSOC = waste away from the classroom." → Half-right, and the wrong half. SPS does spend a larger share on MSOC (17.8% vs ~15% for large peers — actually a ~$33M gap, larger than the flyer's $19.1M). But the entire excess is purchased services, much of it mandated instruction; the classroom-materials half of MSOC (supplies) is flat and underspent 20–40% every year (§9). "MSOC is high" is true; "MSOC is low-impact overhead" is not.
  • The two "extra staff" claims count the same dollars twice. → Cannot be summed. The flyer offers "$19M → 79+39+22 = 140 FTEs" (from matching peers' pay/benefits share) and "redirect purchased services → 197+98+22 = 317 FTEs" as if they were independent opportunities. They are the same pool of money: Seattle's entire MSOC excess over peers is purchased services (its supplies are below the peer level, §9), so the $19M staffing-gap dollars are a subset of the purchased-services dollars. The "hundreds of additional staff" banner invites the reader to add 140 + 317 ≈ 450 FTEs of slack that does not exist.
  • The "redirect to add staff" story contradicts the flyer's own fund-balance story. → Internally inconsistent. The flyer's back half documents a structural operating deficit (spending exceeded revenue in 5 of the last 6 years, −$23.7M in 2024–25) and a fund balance falling from 15.4% to 8.2% of spending — a district drawing down reserves. That is the opposite of a district sitting on hundreds of FTEs of redirectable slack; if that slack existed it would already be closing the gap. A purchased-services-to-staff swap is roughly cost-neutral, so it frees no new money — while the flyer's separate pitch to add 47+24+5 net-new FTEs on rising levy revenue would deepen the very deficit it flags. Put the other way, using the flyer's own blended cost of ~$136K per FTE ($19M ÷ 140 positions): closing the 2024–25 operating gap (−$23.7M) would mean eliminating ~175 positions, not adding them — ~95 in 2023–24 (−$13.0M) and ~307 in the worst recent year (2022–23, −$41.6M). The deficit the flyer documents points toward staff reductions to balance the operating budget — the opposite of the hundreds of additions it projects. The scarcity story and the abundance story cannot both be true.

Two flyer claims are well-founded and should be conceded: SPS comes in under its amended budget yet runs growing operating deficits with a falling fund balance (§2), and purchased services does overshoot its own budget line by 20–30% three years running (§4). The honest critique is narrow and specific — not a blanket "the District overspends," and not the flyer's "mystery / convert-to-hundreds-of-staff" story.

2Does the District "overspend"? Not at the top line.

The first thing to settle, because it shapes every other answer. Against the final (mid-year amended) budget, actual General-Fund spending lands under budget every single year — the "POSITIVE variance" on OSPI's Budgetary Comparison Schedule. What has changed is the operating margin: revenues minus expenditures swung from comfortable surpluses to a −$24M to −$66M deficit in the last four years. The District isn't busting its budget; it adopted budgets it can no longer fund, and is drawing down fund balance.

Why "overspending by huge amounts" still rings true

Against the original adopted budget (the August F-195, before mid-year amendments), several lines — purchased services chief among them — blow past plan by tens of millions and are then "trued up" by amending the budget upward mid-year. So the final budget always ends up just above actual. If the reference point is the budget the Board adopted in the summer, the overspend is real; it is just absorbed by amendments before the year closes.

3Where the money goes — and the "82%" reality

The flyer's 81.8% figure is exactly right: salaries + benefits (objects 2/3/4) were 81.8% of the General Fund in 2024–25 and have held in the 82–85% band for a decade. The story is in the other ~18% — and in which non-salary object is growing.

5How does Seattle compare? (indexed to 2013–14 = 100)

The right question is whether this is a Seattle problem or a Washington problem. Indexing each district's General-Fund actuals to its 2013–14 level isolates the growth rate and removes size. We benchmarked Seattle against the 13 next-largest WA districts and the statewide total.

The honest headline: the purchased-services / MSOC surge is statewide

Nearly every large district roughly doubled MSOC and grew Purchased Services even faster, post-McCleary. Seattle (+102% MSOC, +141% Purchased Services) sits above the statewide average (+92% / +128%) but is not the outlier — Federal Way (+182%), Everett (+171%), Kent and Northshore (+164%) all grew Purchased Services faster. So "rising contracted spending" is a sector-wide pattern, not evidence that Seattle alone is mismanaged.

Where Seattle does stand out is the tilt between contracts and classroom compensation. Seattle's Purchased Services grew +141% while its Teaching compensation grew only +86% — a 55-point gap. Statewide the same gap is 34 points (128% vs 94%), and Seattle's teaching-compensation growth is on the low end of the peer group (Lake Washington +130%, Edmonds +112%, Puyallup +115%). In plain terms: relative to peers, Seattle shifted toward buying services and away from growing classroom payroll faster than most.

The enrollment asterisk — why Seattle's "mid-pack" is worse than it looks

This compares total dollars. But Seattle lost students over the period (−4%, and −9% off its 2019–20 peak), while several faster-growing peers (Lake Washington, Northshore, Federal Way, Kent) added students. Seattle grew MSOC as fast as a statewide average that includes many growing districts — while shrinking. On a per-student basis, Seattle's MSOC and purchased-services growth would rank materially higher than the totals here suggest. A clean per-pupil peer comparison is the natural next step for pressing this point.

The pattern in one sentence

Inside the Teaching activity, the District budgets ~$66M of classroom salaries & benefits it does not spend (vacancies, turnover, unfilled positions) and ~$13M of classroom supplies it does not buy — and simultaneously spends ~$16M over budget on purchased services. The summary nets these to roughly zero; the classroom composition shift is invisible above this level of detail.

7What is the purchased-services money? (the flyer's "specific contracts")

Three big buckets

  • Pupil transportation — ~$57M (NCES 519, "student transportation purchased from another"). The yellow-bus contract. It jumped from ~$39M (2019–20) to ~$58M (2022–23) and has stayed there — a step-change in the per-rider price of the contract, not enrollment.
  • Districtwide Support — ~$48M (program 97): utilities ($16.5M), property/liability insurance ($8.9M, +523% since 2013–14), building maintenance ($6.3M), IT, and Board of Directors $6.0M (legal/consulting). This is the "removed from the classroom" category in the literal sense — central operations. It has grown but is a smaller share of the growth than people assume.
  • Special-education contracted instruction & tuition — ~$42M, coded to the Teaching activity (so it is "classroom" spending delivered by non-employees). This is the single largest budget overrun in the District and the subject of §7.

Why this matters for the return-on-investment question

This is not low-impact spending far from students — it is core instruction, delivered at a premium by contractors and private schools because the District is structurally short of in-house special-education capacity. The right question isn't "cut the contracts," it's: what would it cost to build the in-house special-education staffing that these tuition + contracted-staff dollars are renting? That is the highest-leverage place where "invest in programmatic/staffing levels instead of MSOC" is literally true — the money is already being spent on instruction; the question is whether buying it is cheaper or dearer than staffing it, and whether outcomes differ.

9Staffing trends and the educational program

From the S-275 payroll record (12 years), corroborated by the +107% classified-salary growth above:

  • Teacher FTE is flat-to-down; paraeducators surged. Aide headcount rose 873 → 1,469 (+68%) and aide FTE 598 → 1,012 (+69%) while classroom-teacher FTE was flat or declining. The teacher-to-aide FTE ratio compressed from 4.8 : 1 to 2.9 : 1. That is the classified-salary line (+107%) outrunning the certificated line (+90%).
  • Per-teacher cost rose sharply. Salary per 1.0 FTE for an elementary homeroom teacher went $69.5k → $113.1k (+63%), with step-jumps post-McCleary (2018–19) and the 2022–23 COLA. Compensation growth is price (per-FTE pay), not headcount.
  • Student–teacher ratio is stable (~17:1). The educational-program shift is not larger classes; it is a changing mix — more adults who are paraeducators and contractors, relatively fewer (and much costlier) certificated teachers, and a growing slice of instruction purchased rather than staffed.

10MSOC: where it's high, where it isn't

"MSOC" (materials, supplies & operating costs ≈ objects 5 + 7 + 8) is 17.8% of the General Fund, up from a 12.4% low — but the composition matters enormously:

  • The classroom-materials half of MSOC is not high — it's under-used. Supplies/materials (object 5) is flat in dollars (+10% over 11 yrs, i.e. down per student) and is underspent against budget by 20–40% every single year ($21.8M unspent in 2024–25 against a $55.3M budget). There is budgeted classroom-materials money the District is not deploying.
  • The whole MSOC increase is Purchased Services — transportation, central operating costs, and contracted special-education instruction (§6–7). If the concern is "above-normal MSOC," that is the precise address.
  • Lower-return candidates to examine first (least connected to instruction): the property/liability insurance line (+523%), the Board's $6M purchased-services line, and the step-up in the transportation contract's unit price. The special-education tuition/contracted-staff dollars, by contrast, are instruction — re-investment there means building staffing capacity, not cutting service.
