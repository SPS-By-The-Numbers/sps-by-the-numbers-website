Seattle Public Schools — Transportation Funding Analysis

STARS formula inputs, F-196 actuals, and route detail data · 2013–2026 · Data: OSPI quarterly reports, 1026A allocations, SPS enrollment

Funding Gap

Key Takeaways
  • Transportation costs blew past budget by $10.8M in 2022-23 and no changes have been made since then to bring costs down — the $12.9M overrun in 2023-24 alone equaled the entire general fund deficit that year. ↓ Budget vs. Actual
  • Contracted costs jumped 50% from $40M to $60M between 2019-20 and 2022-23 and have not come back down. → Contract Overages tab
  • SPS self-funded $23M of transportation in 2024-25 — the STARS formula covered 62 cents of every dollar spent. ↓ F-196 detail
  • Basic ridership fell 27% post-COVID and has not recovered, costing ~$8–9M/year in lost formula value. ↓ Riders by program
  • Special Ed now accounts for 59% of all routes (up from 49%), but the formula values sped riders at 1/6th the rate of Basic. ↓ Composition shift
  • The D.2 prior-year cap created a “cap trap” in 2021-22 that suppressed the allocation by $34M in a single year. ↓ Key inflection points
  • Common proposals for savings (eliminating option school routes, converting HCC to neighborhood schools, growing walk zones) show little or negative net benefit in Shaky AI modeling once formula penalties and redistribution costs are accounted for. → Shaky AI Analyses tab

The Structural Shift: Where the Money Goes

The charts below show three views of the same structural transformation: SPS’s transportation system has shifted from a Basic-dominated operation to a Special Ed-dominated one, without a corresponding reduction in total cost. This is the operational engine behind the funding gap.

Basic program — shrinking on every dimension. Riders fell from 12,546 (2017-18) to 9,214 (2025-26), a 27% decline. Routes fell from 225 to 192. Buses fell from 145 to 117. Pre-COVID, Basic riders outnumbered Special Ed riders 5.6-to-1; today the ratio is 3.6-to-1. This collapse is the primary driver of the STARS formula gap: the Basic Program coefficient (0.665) is the dominant term, and losing ~3,300 Basic riders reduced the expected allocation by approximately $8–9M annually.

Special Ed — growing in routes and buses, flat in riders. Sped riders have been remarkably stable: 2,236 in 2017-18, 2,542 in 2025-26 (+14%). But sped routes grew from 344 to 369 and sped buses from 197 to 207, both crossing above Basic in recent years. The story is not more students needing transport — it is each student requiring more individualized routing. Riders per sped route fell from 6.5 to 6.9, and each new route costs ~$100K/year to operate while recovering only ~$440 in STARS formula value.

Other programs — collapsing. The Gifted (HCC), Early Ed, Homeless, and Bilingual programs collectively dropped from 3,005 riders and 140 routes in 2017-18 to 1,640 riders and 60 routes today. Most of this is the Gifted program shrinking from 2,580 to 1,301 riders post-COVID. These riders count in the Special CSPC at the low 0.11 coefficient — their loss has minimal formula impact but reflects a broader enrollment and program contraction.

Why this matters for costs: Total buses in the fleet have barely changed — 465 in 2017-18 vs. 380 today. But the mix has shifted: Basic buses (which carry 50+ students each at ~$575/day) shrank, while Sped buses (which carry ~7 students each at a similar or higher daily rate) grew. The fleet is running at lower utilization with higher per-student costs.

Why this matters for funding: The STARS formula rewards Basic ridership at 6× the rate of Special Ed ridership. Every structural shift from Basic toward Sped — whether driven by enrollment, IEP compliance, or program changes — simultaneously raises operating costs and reduces formula recovery. SPS is spending more per student while the formula pays less per student.

The $23M gap in one sentence: SPS lost ~3,300 Basic riders (costing ~$8.7M in formula value), gained ~300 Sped riders (recovering ~$0.3M), added ~25 net Sped routes (costing ~$2.5M to operate), and saw its contracted rate increase ~50% post-COVID (adding ~$15M in base costs). The formula captured almost none of the cost increase.

Key Inflection Points

2021-22 Cap Trap (−$27.7M): Formula calculated $53.9M but COVID-year D.2 of $18.3M capped the allocation at $20M. The mechanism designed to prevent overpayment became the instrument of maximum underpayment.

2022-23 Worst Year (−$37.1M): SPS spent $65.3M while D.2 used 2021-22’s partial-recovery figure as ceiling. SPS self-funded 57 cents of every transportation dollar.

2023-24 Route Restructuring (+76 net sped routes): SPS added 141 new Special Ed routes and dropped 65, a near-complete rebuild of the sped routing system. Special Ed routes grew from 223 to 299 (+34%) while sped riders grew from 1,881 to 2,290 (+22%). Riders per sped route fell from 8.4 to 7.7 and has declined every year since. The formula recovered approximately $420K of the ~$7.6M in new route operating costs — about 5 cents per dollar.

2024-25 Partial Recovery (−$23.0M): D.2 now reflects real expenditures. The cap is no longer artificially low. The remaining gap is structural: Basic ridership has not recovered to pre-COVID levels (~9,200 vs ~18,000 in 2019-20), costing approximately $23M in formula allocation annually.

2022-25 Budget Disconnect (+$27.3M cumulative overrun): Independent of the STARS funding gap, SPS’s own transportation budget stopped tracking reality. In 2022-23, the adopted budget was $54.5M against $65.3M actual (+$10.8M over). Rather than correcting upward, the 2023-24 budget was cut to $47.2M — while actual spend hit $60.0M (+$12.9M over, the largest single-year overrun on record). By 2024-25 the budget partially corrected to $57.1M but still fell $3.6M short. Over three years the district spent $27.3M more than it budgeted for transportation — an amount roughly equal to the entire 2024-25 general fund deficit of $23.7M. This occurred while contracted costs were rising above board-authorized ceilings, Basic ridership was declining 27%, and the route structure was being rebuilt. The budget was not adjusted to reflect any of these known changes. The result was that the general fund absorbed $27.3M in unplanned transportation costs over three years with no corresponding budget authority, revenue offset, or service reduction to compensate.

The D.2 Lag: STARS uses prior-year F-196 actuals as the expenditure cap. When costs jump sharply, D.2 understates what SPS spent — tightening the cap at the worst moment. The restructuring in 2023-24 raised the cost base that D.2 will reflect in future years, partially closing the lag.

Contract Overages

NCES 519 actual spend compared to board-authorized contract ceilings. SPS does not operate its own bus fleet — virtually all transportation spending (95–97% of F-196 total) flows through NCES 519 (“Student Transportation Services Purchased from Another”) to outside contractors.

Why NCES 519 rather than Activity/Program codes? NCES 519 isolates the pure contracted-services spend — payments to First Student, Zum, EverDriven, and other outside vendors — which is directly comparable to board-authorized contract ceilings. Activity codes 51–59 (excl. 58) capture total transportation expenditure including internal SPS costs (admin salaries, supplies), which run $0–$2.7M above NCES 519 and are not subject to the same contract authorization process. For the question “did SPS spend more than the board authorized on contracted services?” NCES 519 is the correct numerator. Data availability: NCES 519 actuals are only available from FY 2019-20 onward. The longer expenditure history in the Funding Gap tab (back to 2017-18) uses Activity 51–59.
Data limitations: NCES 519 actuals are from a district financial report (fiscal year end). Board authorization amounts are from public board meeting agendas. The “gap above ceiling” figures assume the full NTE was spent; if alt transport was only partially utilized, the gap above the bus-only contract ceiling would be larger, and the split between the two contract types is unknown. The Cost Decomposition
How $40M became $60M — three structural layers

Layer 1: Base contract reset (+$5.2M/yr)
The new dual contract (First Student + Zum) authorized $45.2M/yr vs. the prior ~$40M/yr. This 12.5% increase reflects post-COVID driver wage inflation and the addition of a second contractor. This layer is permanent and baked into the current contract.

Layer 2: Alternative transport ($0–$10.5M/yr — actual spend unknown)
A new contract layer for rideshare/van services (EverDriven, HopSkipDrive, etc.) for students with individualized transport needs. The NTE grew from $8.45M (2022-23) to $10.45M (2023-25). We do not know what was actually spent within these ceilings — actual utilization could be anywhere from minimal to the full NTE. Without vendor-level NCES 519 data, the true spend is unverifiable from public records. These services generate zero STARS formula value.

Layer 3: Unexplained overage (+$4.4–$9.4M/yr)
Actual NCES 519 spend exceeds the sum of located board authorizations in every year. The 2022-23 gap ($9.35M) was the largest, declining to $4.35M in 2023-24 and 2024-25. This likely reflects: mid-year contract amendments not yet located in board records, spending under the Superintendent’s delegated procurement authority, and/or variable pricing provisions in the bus contract that allow costs above the base NTE.

The decomposition: The entire $18.4M increase decomposes into two factors. Route count fell by 48, which at the old per-route cost should have saved $3.1M. Instead, the per-route rate jumped from $64,410 to $99,816, adding $21.5M. Net: +$18.4M. There is no volume increase to explain the spending growth — fewer routes, fewer riders, fewer miles, roughly the same bus count, shorter distances.

What changed — four identifiable factors: (1) The BAFO rate increase from ~$500 to ~$575/route-day (+15% on the $42.3M base = +$6.3M). (2) The alternative transport layer (EverDriven, HopSkipDrive, Zum rideshare) that did not exist pre-COVID, authorized at up to $10.45M/yr ($0–10.5M — we have no data on actual utilization of these contracts; the authorization exists but actual spend could be minimal or near the ceiling). (3) The route mix shift toward sped (+15 sped routes, −17 basic) with sped routes costing more per route due to aides and smaller vehicles (+$0.8M). (4) Growth in “Other” program buses (Gifted, Homeless, Early Ed, Bilingual: 33 → 56) at ~$100K/yr each (+$2.3M). The BAFO rate increase, route mix shift, and Other bus growth are grounded in data and account for $9.4M. The alt transport layer could account for the remaining $9M — or it could be much less, with the balance sitting in bus contract overages or excess-hour charges. Without vendor-level NCES 519 data, the split is unknown. The per-route rate looks like a 55% jump, but it is not a single cause; it is the sum of a modest contract rate increase, a large new service layer, and a fleet mix shift.

The uncomfortable comparison: SPS is running 9% fewer route-miles and carrying 40% fewer riders while paying 44% more. The service is objectively smaller; only the price is larger.

Remaining Gaps & Data Limitations
What we know, what we searched, and what’s still missing

✅ Resolved: Per-route-day rates. The BAFO rate schedules confirm volume-tiered pricing at ~$575/route/day at SPS volumes. The contract is a rate schedule, not a fixed fee. This explains how actual costs can exceed the $45.2M NTE projection when SPS deploys more routes than anticipated.

✅ Resolved: Contract variable pricing. The BAFO confirms the pricing model — costs scale with route count at tiered rates, plus excess hours, mileage, and field trips as variable add-ons. No separate amendment is needed when costs exceed the NTE; the rate schedule governs.

❌ Searched, not found: Missing board authorizations. Searched all SPS board meeting agendas from January–June 2023 (10 meetings). No transportation contract amendments, rate adjustments, or additional NTE authorizations were found. Board Policy 6220 delegates procurement authority to the Superintendent for amendments up to $500,000, but the $4.35–$9.35M gaps far exceed this threshold. The overages likely flow through the variable rate schedule rather than requiring separate board action.

❌ Not available: NCES 519 actuals pre-2019. No NCES 519 data exists prior to FY 2020 in our data sources.

❌ Not available: Vendor-level NCES 519 breakdown. We cannot determine how much of the $60–63M goes to First Student vs. Zum vs. the alternative transport providers. This would require accounts payable detail from a public records request.

What the Comparison Shows

Seattle places dead last on every operational efficiency measure in OSPI’s 21-district cohort. The cost-per-rider gap is not marginal — Seattle’s $8,652 is 2.4× Tacoma’s and 3.1× Federal Way’s. The cost-per-route-mile chart shows this is a supply-side problem: Seattle’s network costs more to operate per mile driven, regardless of ridership.

Tacoma is the most revealing comparison. Both districts are urban, both have school choice, and both lost ~30% of basic riders across COVID. But Tacoma tracks its STARS allocation closely while Seattle’s expenditure runs $20–30M above. The gap between them is not geography or COVID.

Federal Way provides the efficiency benchmark. It barely lost riders, runs 127 basic riders per bus, and keeps cost per rider at $2,835. Federal Way is suburban, so direct comparison is imperfect — but it demonstrates what’s achievable within the same STARS formula.

Shaky AI Analyses

⚠ Important disclaimer before reading anything below. These analyses are preliminary, based on incomplete data, and depend heavily on assumptions that have not been validated against SPS operational data. They are intended to generate hypotheses for further investigation, not to support policy decisions. Several key inputs — per-route costs, student address distributions, walk zone sizes, IEP transport obligations, and actual count-day attendance — are estimated or assumed rather than measured. Results could change substantially with better data. Do not cite these figures without independent verification.

Background

Global Assumptions Underlying All Analyses
These assumptions apply across multiple questions below. Each represents a potential source of error.
  • Operating cost per route: ~$571/day from F-196 average, consistent with BAFO contracted rates of ~$575/day at SPS volume. Special Ed routes with aides or specialized vehicles almost certainly cost more. Alt transport (EverDriven, HopSkipDrive) costs are separate and not included in per-route estimates.
  • Per-route ridership derived from quarterly summary On Buses ÷ route count (~51.5 riders/route for Basic). This assumes ridership is uniformly distributed across routes, which is unlikely.
  • Walk zone absorption rates (what share of students live close enough to their new school to not need a bus) are estimated at 25–75% with no supporting address-level data.
  • New neighborhood routes assumed to average ~25 students each — roughly half the current option school average — reflecting geographic dispersion. Actual routing would depend on address data.
  • Transit pass cost assumed at $900 (not verified against current SPS transit pass agreements)/student/year. Actual SPS transit pass cost not verified.
  • Geographic distribution of option and HCC school students assumed roughly city-wide. In reality, HCC enrollment likely skews toward certain neighborhoods.
  • Formula calculations use 2025-26 coefficients throughout. Coefficients change annually — results would differ in other years.
  • Gifted route riders assumed to count in Special Program CSPC per OSPI General Instructions. This has not been independently confirmed against the 1022E form itself.
  • Count-day consistency assumed at ~80% (students present on count day). Actual SPS attendance data not reviewed.
  • Route length and operational scheduling constraints (driver hours, school start windows) are not modeled except where noted.

2021-22 is a severe outlier. The COVID year produced a regression on abnormal ridership data: Basic coefficient halved (0.394 vs ~0.67 in normal years), Special tripled (0.303 vs ~0.11), and the land area coefficient went negative (−0.169). The constant jumped to 10.58 — nearly 2.8 points above any other year. This is also the year the formula calculated $53.9M for SPS while D.2 capped the actual allocation at $20M.

Basic coefficient range: 0.394–0.704. The marginal value of a Basic rider (used in the walk zone analysis) ranges from roughly $1,500 to $2,900 depending on the year’s coefficients and current rider counts. The $2,650 figure cited in that analysis is 2025-26 only.

Special coefficient range: 0.092–0.303. In normal years (excluding 2021-22) the Special coefficient sits between 0.092 and 0.124 — consistently about one-sixth of the Basic coefficient. The 2021-22 inversion (Special > Basic) is a statistical artifact of COVID-era data and should not be treated as representing a real policy choice.

Analyses

Six explorations of commonly proposed changes and their estimated budget impact. Sorted by largest absolute impact. Each includes data sources, assumptions, verification steps, and open questions.

1Exploration: Does shrinking or expanding the walk zone increase or decrease STARS funding?
▼
Est. budget impact: $-7.2M to +$7.4M/yrConfidence: LOW
🔎 Tentative Finding
Shrinking the walk zone (busing more students) appears to generate positive net returns because the marginal formula value per added Basic rider (~$2,650/yr at 2025-26 coefficients) substantially exceeds the marginal operating cost of adding stops to existing routes (~$51–640/student/yr). The range is wide because it depends heavily on whether new riders can be served by adding stops to existing routes or require entirely new routes. Expanding the walk zone is negative in all scenarios — formula loss per removed rider exceeds operating savings even when full route elimination is achieved ($99,925 saved vs ~$127,000 lost in formula per route eliminated (48 riders/route × $2,650)).
📊 Data & Methods
Formula marginal value computed analytically from 2025-26 1026A coefficients. Operating cost estimated from F-196 total expenditure ÷ routes ÷ days (~$571/route/day). BAFO contracted rate at SPS volume is ~$575/route/day, consistent with this estimate. Stop addition cost estimated at $40/day driver time ÷ 10 students per stop = ~$51/student/yr marginal cost. New route cost ~$1,960/student/yr.
⚠ Key Assumptions
  • 70–90% of walk-zone-adjacent students can be served by adding stops to existing routes rather than new routes — this depends entirely on whether existing routes pass near those students, which is unknown without GIS analysis
  • Stop addition cost estimated at $40/day extra driver time, divided across ~10 students per stop — not verified against SPS driver contracts
  • Route length and timing constraints are not modeled — if existing routes are at capacity, stop additions may trigger new routes instead
  • Count-day consistency assumed at 80%
✅ Steps to Verify
  • SPS has address-level enrollment data and GIS stop locations — model which walk-zone students are near existing routes vs. would require new routes
  • Review current route timing windows for slack capacity to add stops
  • Check state minimum walk zone requirements (SPS cannot set zones below state minimums)
  • Review driver contract constraints on route length and shift time
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • The entire positive result depends on existing routes passing near walk-zone students — if they don’t, most would require new routes, reducing the net benefit substantially (though it remains positive even at 50% new routes)
  • Adding stops may push routes past school start/end timing windows, triggering new routes rather than stop additions
  • The average distance term in the formula decreases slightly as shorter-distance stops are added — this partially offsets the CBPC gain (modeled as small but not verified)
2Exploration: What if option and HCC schools were made into neighborhood schools?
▼
Est. budget impact: −$5.6M to −$7.2M/yrConfidence: LOW
🔎 Tentative Finding
Converting option and HCC schools to neighborhood attendance-area schools makes SPS’s financial position worse in every scenario tested. The operating cost of building new neighborhood-scale routes (many short routes, few students each) exceeds savings from eliminating option routes. Net losses range from roughly −$5.6M to −$7.2M depending on walk zone absorption assumptions (25–50%). The formula effect is mixed but generally negative due to loss of Gifted CSPC riders and the dispersion of students across many short new routes.
📊 Data & Methods
13 option/HCC schools, ~5,350 students total. Route counts from Fall quarterly detail pages. Operating cost at $571/route/day. Walk zone absorption modeled at 25–50% of the ~5,350 redistributed option/HCC students: meaning we assume 25–50% would end up within walking distance of their assigned neighborhood school and not need a bus. This range was chosen arbitrarily as a plausible mid-range; it is not derived from address-level data or any prior SPS study. New neighborhood routes assumed at 25 students/route average. Formula uses 2025-26 coefficients.
⚠ Key Assumptions
  • Walk zone absorption (what % of redistributed students end up within walking distance of their new neighborhood school) assumed at 25–50% — the single most consequential unknown, with no SPS address-level data to support it
  • New neighborhood routes average 25 students each — roughly half the current option school average — reflecting geographic dispersion after redistribution
  • Geographic distribution of option school families assumed roughly city-wide — HCC enrollment likely skews toward certain neighborhoods, which could significantly change walk zone absorption
  • Sped transport assumed unchanged (follows the student via IEP)
✅ Steps to Verify
  • SPS has address-level enrollment data that would allow actual walk zone calculation to each student’s neighborhood school — this would replace the 25–50% assumption with a real number
  • Routing software (Versatrans) could model actual new route requirements
  • Review legal requirements for HCC transportation under RCW 28A.185 (state highly capable statute)
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • Walk zone absorption dominates the result — shifting the assumption by 10 percentage points (e.g. from 35% to 45% of redistributed students living within walking distance of their new school) shifts the net impact by ~$1–2M
  • HCC schools are centrally located relative to a city-wide draw — students travel from across Seattle precisely because the program is not neighborhood-based. Walk zone absorption upon redistribution to neighborhood schools could be lower than the city average, since HCC families may live far from both the HCC school and their neighborhood school
  • Political and legal feasibility of eliminating option programs is entirely outside this analysis
3Exploration: Why did the 2023-24 restructuring add so many routes without proportional ridership growth?
▼
Ongoing annual cost: ~−$7.2M vs. $420K formula recoveryConfidence: MEDIUM
🔎 Tentative Finding
The 2023-24 expansion was almost entirely Special Ed routes (+76 net: 141 new, 65 dropped). Basic routes were essentially unchanged (+1 net). New Special Ed routes have longer average distances (2.49 mi vs 2.20 mi for continued routes) and lower student counts. The pattern — district-wide, shallow increases at 53+ schools, most gaining just 1–2 routes — is what IEP compliance expansion would look like, but no board record, OCR correspondence, or policy directive has been found to confirm this hypothesis. The formula recovered approximately $420K of the ~$7.6M in new route operating costs (~5 cents per dollar). Riders per sped route have fallen every year since: 8.4 → 7.7 → 7.3 → 7.0.
📊 Data & Methods
Route IDs from Fall quarterly detail pages 2022-23 and 2023-24. Continuity analysis: 390 route IDs survived unchanged, 104 dropped, 182 new. New routes identified by ID not present in prior year. Distance averages from route detail distance field. Formula recovery calculated from CSPC change × 2025-26 coefficients.
⚠ Key Assumptions
  • Route ID changes treated as genuinely new routes, not renumbering — the Ingraham example shows 8 of 9 route IDs were retired and replaced even where net count changed only slightly, so this may overstate “newness”
  • The compliance interpretation is inferred from the data pattern — no SPS policy document, IEP audit, or board directive has been reviewed
  • Operating cost per sped route assumed at the district average ($571/day) — sped routes likely cost more due to aides and specialized vehicles
✅ Steps to Verify
  • Ask SPS transportation staff what drove the 2023-24 expansion — was there an IEP audit, OCR complaint, or board directive?
  • Review SPS board meeting minutes from 2022-23 for transportation policy changes
  • Check whether OCR or OSPI issued compliance correspondence related to special ed transportation
  • Check if the route ID system was intentionally rebuilt (explaining mass ID retirement) vs. IDs changed for operational reasons
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • The compliance hypothesis is plausible but unverified — the expansion could reflect enrollment growth, a new contractor, building moves (Mercer TEMP CONST), or other factors
  • We cannot tell from route data alone whether new routes serve genuinely new transport needs or replace informal arrangements (parent reimbursement, contracted individual runs) previously off the route detail system
  • The mass route ID retirement at Ingraham (1 of 9 IDs survived) suggests a system-wide routing rebuild, which could mean “new” routes are operationally the same routes with new numbers
4Exploration: What happens to STARS funding when Basic riders shift to other transport categories?
▼
~−$1,700 to −$2,650 per shifted student/yrConfidence: MEDIUM
🔎 Tentative Finding
Moving a student off a Basic route always reduces STARS funding. How much depends on where they go. The Basic Program coefficient (0.665) is the dominant term in the formula. Any shift that removes a rider from the Basic CBPC count incurs a formula loss of ~$2,650/student/yr. If the student gains a count in the Special CSPC (Special Ed, Gifted, Early Ed, Homeless — all count in CSPC per OSPI instructions), approximately $948/student is recovered, for a net loss of ~$1,700/student. If the student leaves the formula entirely (transit pass, walk zone, parent transport), the full $2,650/student is lost with no recovery.

Practical implication: There is no category shift that improves funding. Every destination category is worth less to the formula than Basic. The formula is calibrated to maximize value for students on standard Basic routes — any specialization or removal from district buses reduces the allocation.

Scale example: Basic “On Buses” ridership declined from 12,546 (2017-18) to 9,214 (2025-26) — a loss of ~3,300 riders. At the marginal formula value of ~$2,650/rider, this costs approximately $8–9M annually. Meanwhile, the CBPC formula input declined even more steeply (20,708 → 9,195), reflecting broader enrollment and program changes beyond just On Buses counts. The full CBPC-driven formula loss is ~$27M, which is the dominant driver of the $23M funding gap.

⚠ Key Assumptions
  • CSPC gain per student ($948) computed at current CSPC=4,256 using 2025-26 coefficients — this changes at different rider counts and in different years
  • Gifted, Early Ed, and Homeless riders assumed to count in CSPC per OSPI General Instructions — not independently confirmed against the 1022E submission form
  • The −$1,700/student net figure holds only when the destination category adds to CSPC; it assumes rider counts stay in the formula-relevant range
✅ Steps to Verify
  • Obtain OSPI Form 1022E instructions to confirm how each program type (Gifted, Early Ed, Homeless, Bilingual) is classified in CBPC vs. CSPC
  • Ask SPS transportation staff how they classify riders on their 1022E submission
  • Cross-check the CBPC and CSPC values on the 1026A against the quarterly summary On Buses counts by category
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • The 1022E classification of Gifted riders as CSPC is the single most consequential unverified assumption in this analysis
  • The formula uses annual averages (CBPC = avg of three count days) — students who ride inconsistently contribute less than a full unit to the count
  • The $8–9M “On Buses” estimate uses linearized marginal values. The actual CBPC-driven formula loss (~$27M) is much larger because CBPC declined more steeply than On Buses counts and the log transformation amplifies large declines. The $23M gap reflects both the formula decline and the partial recovery from other inputs.
5Exploration: What happens to transportation costs and funding if SPS consolidates half its schools?
▼
Est. budget impact: −$14M to −$22M/yrConfidence: LOW
🔎 Tentative Finding
Consolidating ~107 schools to ~53 makes the transportation funding picture significantly worse in every scenario tested. The STARS formula drops by $12–17M because the “destinations” input (number of school sites served) is halved. Ridership increases by 4,900–7,000 Basic riders (displaced walkers who now need a bus), partially offsetting the destinations loss, but not enough. Operating costs do not improve — fewer routes but longer routes roughly cancel out, and in aggressive scenarios the longer-route cost actually exceeds the route-elimination savings.
📊 Data & Methods

Formula modeling: EXAL calculated with 2025-26 coefficients. Destinations reduced from 105.75 to 53–58. Average distance increased from 2.16 to 2.8–3.3 miles (based on doubling the service area per school, distance scales ~√2). CBPC increased by 15–25% reflecting displaced walkers becoming riders.

Ridership modeling: ~25,600 students displaced from closed schools. Of those, ~8,200 estimated current walkers (32% walk rate). 60–85% of displaced walkers assumed to need a bus at their new, farther school. 5–15% of existing riders assumed to opt out due to longer ride times. Net CBPC change: +4,700 to +6,300 riders.

Operating cost modeling: Route count reduced by basic route ratio (~destinations/106) with 1.2× multiplier for longer routes. Sped routes reduced only 10–20% (IEP mandates follow students). Per-route cost scaled linearly with average distance increase.

⚠ Key Assumptions
  • Walk rate assumed at 32% across closed schools — actual walk rate varies enormously by school (0% at option schools, 40%+ at some neighborhood elementaries). A school-by-school closure plan would change this significantly.
  • Average distance increase modeled as ~√2 (×1.3–1.5) based on doubling the service area per school. Actual increase depends entirely on which schools close and the resulting attendance boundaries.
  • Sped routes assumed to decrease only 10–20% because IEP mandates follow the student. In practice, consolidation could increase sped routes if students are now farther from their placement school.
  • Per-route cost scaled linearly with distance — a simplification; actual cost depends on driver hours, not just miles.
  • No transition costs modeled (temporary busing during moves, building modifications, etc.).
  • Enrollment assumed constant — consolidation may accelerate enrollment loss to private/charter schools, worsening ridership.
✅ Steps to Verify
  • Model specific closure scenarios (not just “half the schools”) with actual attendance boundary redrawing and student address data
  • Use routing software to estimate actual route changes for each scenario
  • Check whether a 3-tier bell schedule (feasible with fewer schools) could offset the formula loss through fleet reduction
  • Review the 2009 SPS school closure experience for actual transportation cost impacts
❓ Open Questions & Possible Misinterpretations
  • “Destinations” in the STARS formula counts unique school sites by OSPI school code, not route endpoints. Co-located schools with separate codes count as separate destinations (OSPI General Reporting Instructions, p. 3: “The STARS GIS system provides separate destination numbers for co-located schools based on assigned school codes.”). SPS destinations = 105.75 (2024-25), consistent with ~107 school sites. This means halving the number of schools would roughly halve the destinations input, confirming the −$17M formula penalty estimate.
  • If consolidation enables a 3-tier bell schedule, the $10–15M in bus fleet savings could partially or fully offset the formula loss — but that is a separate policy decision, not an inherent effect of consolidation
  • The model assumes all displaced students attend SPS — if consolidation drives enrollment to private schools, both ridership and per-pupil funding decline further
  • School consolidation savings on building operations and staffing are substantially smaller than commonly assumed. Our own analysis using OSPI F-196 and S275 data estimates $630K–$678K per year per school in savings, against which closure-related transition costs of $282K–$322K/yr reduce the net to roughly $308K–$396K/yr per school for the first two years — compared to an SPS estimate of $1.5M/school (see our analysis). At those figures, building savings would be comparable to, not larger than, the transportation effects modeled here.
  • The STARS formula is recalibrated annually — a mass consolidation affecting the statewide regression could change the coefficients themselves, making any projection unreliable beyond 1–2 years
6Variation: Transportation impact of closing 17–21 schools (the “FAB 4” and similar near-term closure scenarios)
▼

A near-term closure of 17–21 schools (107 → 86–90) produces a net transportation loss of $5.1M–$7.7M/yr.
